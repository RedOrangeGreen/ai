UK Oct 2026 Budget Predictions

Autumn Budget — Wednesday 28 October 2026 · Chancellor John Healey

What this Budget has to solve

The Chancellor faces three competing pressures at once: raising enough revenue to meet the Government's fiscal targets, supporting economic growth, and avoiding further pressure on household finances. A measure can be widely discussed without ever being announced.

  • Settled — already committed or explicitly ruled out.
  • Plausible — a serious candidate supported by current reporting or professional analysis.
  • Possible — in play, but politically difficult, expensive or uncertain.
  • Unlikely — speculative, or more likely deferred to a later fiscal event.
These are reasoned assessments, not official forecasts. They draw on published reporting and professional tax analysis, and they are not confirmed government policy. The definitive position emerges when the Chancellor delivers the Budget on 28 October 2026. Nothing here is financial advice.

In detail

Income tax: a freeze is more likely than a rate increase

With thresholds committed to April 2031, rising wages alone pull more income into tax and move taxpayers into higher bands — fiscal drag, without an announced rate rise. Expect the freeze to hold rather than the basic or higher rates to move.

Could the personal allowance rise to £15,000?

It would mean up to £2,430 more income free of tax, a maximum of £486 a year for a basic-rate taxpayer. The cost to the Treasury is substantial, and a Chancellor rebuilding headroom faces real difficulty financing a giveaway of that size.

A new 50% band?

It could raise revenue while leaving headline rates intact, but the Government would have to weigh the effect on incentives, tax planning and the behaviour of high earners.

Capital gains tax: an area worth watching

CGT applies to taxable profits on selling assets such as shares or a second property, with different rules and reliefs by asset and circumstance. Options in circulation: higher rates on some gains, a lower annual exempt amount, changes to business disposal reliefs, revisiting the treatment of inherited assets, and measures affecting higher-value property.

Further adjustments look plausible, particularly if the Chancellor wants revenue without touching income tax rates. A wholesale alignment of CGT with income tax is a bigger step, and PwC considers it less likely given the disruption to investment and taxpayer behaviour. It would be unwise to make a major financial decision on the assumption that the rules will change.

Inheritance tax: tightening reliefs, not abolition

For families and business owners the live question is whether the exemptions and reliefs move, not whether IHT is scrapped. Measures in circulation include extending the seven-year relevant period for potentially exempt gifts to ten years, lifetime limits on certain gifts, and changes to relief for qualifying investments including AIM shares. A social care levy in place of IHT remains speculation, and is thought more likely considered over a longer period than announced immediately.

Property: the mansion tax stands, but watch the edges

The High Value Council Tax Surcharge is already confirmed for April 2028 at £2m and above, charging £2,500–£7,500 a year with the option to defer. Speculation had focused on lowering the threshold to £1.5m; the Financial Times reported on 11 October that the Chancellor has decided against it. For the £1m–£2m band that matters most, the position is unchanged either way. Stamp duty reform and replacing council tax have both been ruled out by the Prime Minister.

Pensions: clarification more likely than restriction

The triple lock is expected to survive. The live pension question is the State Pension rising roughly 3.9% above the £12,570 personal allowance for the first time, and how the commitment to protect pensioners whose only income is that pension will work in practice. Expect clarification of the mechanism rather than a new tax. The tax treatment of pension contributions, salary sacrifice, and pension assets for IHT all sit in the wider debate, but some changes are already scheduled and there is not enough evidence to expect a major new restriction in this Budget.

Energy and fuel: targeted beats universal

A support package costing more than £1bn for lower-income households was reported on 7 October, though approval was not established. The form matters more than the headline: universal support spreads the cost more widely, targeted support directs it. On fuel duty, successive governments have avoided increases for years, making the January 2027 step politically sensitive; a delay is a credible possibility but not a certainty.

Businesses: relief at the bottom, taxes at the top

With corporation tax committed at 25% for large companies, revenue is more likely to come from specific reliefs, allowances or sector taxes than the main rate. Bank executives have met the Chancellor without a clear steer. On business rates, expect targeted relief for smaller retailers, pubs, restaurants and hospitality, building on the confirmed 20% cut from April 2027, with some discussion of shifting burden towards large distribution warehouses.

What could change the picture before Budget day

  • The OBR forecast. Weaker growth, lower receipts or higher debt-servicing costs would leave less room for tax cuts and spending commitments.
  • The cost of borrowing. Higher gilt yields make the fiscal rules harder to meet without new revenue or spending restraint.
  • The political balance. The Government may want to show it is helping with living costs while finding revenue from narrower tax changes.

Where this would land

If you want to narrow this to the changes that actually affect you, the areas that matter most are:

  • Income tax, National Insurance and take-home pay. A freeze holds, with relief for very high earners the open question.
  • Pensions, retirement income and pension tax relief. The triple lock survives; watch how the State Pension interacts with the personal allowance.
  • Inheritance tax, gifts and estate planning. Reliefs tighten before the tax itself is replaced.
  • Property, second homes and capital gains tax. The £2m mansion threshold stands, with targeted changes at the edges.
  • Savings, ISAs, shares and investments. Allowance changes are the live risk rather than new headline rates.
  • The wider economic outlook, interest rates and house prices. These shape everything above.
